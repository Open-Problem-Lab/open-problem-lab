# Medical Oxygen Access And Facility Readiness Gaps In LMICs

## Problem Statement

Medical oxygen is a basic hospital therapy, but public evidence often confuses four different questions: who needs oxygen, whether a facility owns oxygen equipment, whether oxygen is available when needed, and whether clinicians can use it safely with pulse oximetry, power, consumables, and maintenance.

The v0 problem is not "scale medical oxygen." That is too broad and too easy to turn into procurement theater. The sharper problem is:

> Which countries, regions, or facility types have evidence strong enough to distinguish true oxygen access gaps from equipment-count artifacts, so a ministry or funder can choose between procurement, maintenance, market-shaping, tariff, or clinical-quality investments?

## Known Facts

- Verified fact: The 2025 Lancet Global Health Commission on medical oxygen security describes medical oxygen as essential for surgery, emergency care, pneumonia, neonatal care, obstetric care, and many acute conditions, and estimates that roughly 374 million newborns, children, and adults need medical oxygen each year, with 82 percent of these patients in LMICs.
- Verified fact: WHO's 2023 World Health Assembly resolution WHA76.3 recognized oxygen access as a patient-safety and health-system priority and asked Member States to strengthen oxygen systems, including planning, procurement, supply, maintenance, and workforce capacity.
- Verified fact: The WHO-UNICEF Global Report on Assistive Technology estimated that more than 2.5 billion people need at least one assistive product, making assistive technology a larger but less acute comparator problem; oxygen differs because untreated hypoxemia can kill within hours or days.
- Verified fact: WHO estimates rabies causes about 59,000 deaths annually, mostly in Asia and Africa; rabies remains important but has a narrower mortality scale and more established intervention pathway than oxygen-system readiness measurement.

## Uncertain Areas

- How many facilities in each LMIC have functional oxygen sources available during routine and surge conditions.
- Whether public Service Availability and Readiness Assessment, SPA, HeRAMS, or national facility-assessment data can separate equipment presence from uptime, power, consumables, and clinical use.
- Whether oxygen shortage estimates can be tied to specific procurement or maintenance choices rather than broad burden narratives.
- Whether global oxygen financing, COVID-era donations, and market-shaping efforts have reduced shortages or created maintenance liabilities.

## Initial Scope

This pack accepts work on:

- Source inventory for oxygen need, facility readiness, oxygen-system equipment, pulse oximetry, supply reliability, and price or tariff evidence.
- Measure-family reconciliation separating oxygen need, oxygen source availability, oxygen source functionality, oxygen use, and patient outcomes.
- Country or facility-type triage memos identifying where public evidence is strong enough for follow-on analysis.
- Claims about data availability, comparability, failure modes, and decision thresholds.
- Red-team review of procurement overreach, equipment-count misuse, and unsafe clinical extrapolation.
- Field-reality review from biomedical engineering, hospital operations, procurement, or acute-care workflows.

This pack does not accept direct clinical advice, country league tables without replication, or procurement recommendations that bypass domain review.

## Done Condition For V0

The pack is useful when it can produce one reviewed and replicated artifact that classifies at least two LMIC contexts or facility types into:

1. likely priority for oxygen procurement or supply expansion
2. likely priority for maintenance, power, consumables, or workforce fixes
3. not yet distinguishable with public evidence

That artifact must name the decision-maker, data limits, and the specific observation that would overturn the classification.
